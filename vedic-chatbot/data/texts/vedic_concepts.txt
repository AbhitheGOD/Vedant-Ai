DHARMA — The Cosmic Order and Righteous Duty

Sanskrit: धर्म

Etymology: From the root "dhr" meaning to hold, sustain, or uphold. Dharma is that which sustains the universe.

Definition: Dharma is a multifaceted concept encompassing cosmic order (rita), moral law, righteous duty, virtue, and the essential nature of things. It has no single English equivalent.

Key dimensions of Dharma:
1. Sanatana Dharma — the eternal, universal dharma that sustains all existence
2. Varna Dharma — duties according to social role and aptitude
3. Ashrama Dharma — duties according to stage of life (brahmacharya, grihastha, vanaprastha, sannyasa)
4. Svadharma — one's personal dharma according to nature and calling (as taught in Bhagavad Gita 3.35)
5. Yuga Dharma — dharma appropriate to the cosmic age

From the Mahabharata: "Ahimsa paramo dharma" — Non-violence is the highest dharma.
From Manusmriti: The ten pillars of dharma are: patience, forgiveness, self-control, non-stealing, purity, control of senses, intelligence, knowledge, truth, and freedom from anger.

KARMA — The Law of Cause and Effect

Sanskrit: कर्म

Etymology: From the root "kri" meaning to do or make. Karma literally means "action."

Definition: Karma is the universal law of cause and effect applied to intentional action. Every action creates a corresponding reaction — not merely physically, but morally and spiritually.

Types of Karma:
1. Sanchita Karma — accumulated karma from past lives
2. Prarabdha Karma — the portion of karma being experienced in the present life (destiny)
3. Agami/Kriyamana Karma — karma being created in this moment through current actions

Three aspects of karma's operation:
- Karma is created by intention (sankalpa), not just action
- Karma binds through attachment and ego-identification
- Nishkama Karma (desireless action) creates no new binding karma

From Bhagavad Gita (4.17): "The nature of action is very difficult to understand. One must know the nature of action, the nature of forbidden action, and the nature of inaction."

MOKSHA — Liberation

Sanskrit: मोक्ष

Also known as: Mukti, Nirvana (Buddhism), Kaivalya (Yoga), Apavarga (Nyaya)

Definition: Moksha is the ultimate goal of human existence in Vedic thought — liberation from the cycle of birth, death, and rebirth (samsara) and the realization of one's true nature.

The four Purusharthas (aims of human life):
1. Artha — material prosperity
2. Kama — pleasure and desire
3. Dharma — righteous living
4. Moksha — liberation (the highest aim)

Different schools understand moksha differently:
- Advaita Vedanta (Shankara): Moksha is the recognition that Atman = Brahman; the apparent individuality was never real. This is Jivanmukti (liberation while living).
- Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja): Moksha is eternal blissful communion with Ishvara (God) while retaining individual identity.
- Dvaita (Madhva): Moksha is eternal joyful service to Vishnu in Vaikuntha, maintaining individual distinction forever.
- Yoga (Patanjali): Kaivalya — the isolation of pure consciousness from all entanglement with Prakriti.

ATMAN AND BRAHMAN — The Individual and Cosmic Self

Sanskrit: आत्मन् and ब्रह्मन्

Atman: The individual self, the pure consciousness within each being, the witness of all experience. The Atman is described as sat-chit-ananda — being, consciousness, bliss. It is unborn, undying, unchanging.

Brahman: The ultimate reality, the ground of all existence, infinite consciousness. Brahman is described as nirguna (without attributes) at the highest level, but can be approached as saguna Brahman (with attributes) in devotional paths.

The Relationship between Atman and Brahman — the central question of Vedanta:
- Advaita: Atman and Brahman are identical. Aham Brahmasmi ("I am Brahman"). Differences are due to maya (illusion/cosmic ignorance).
- Vishishtadvaita: Atman is part of Brahman, related as body to soul. Real but not absolutely separate.
- Dvaita: Atman and Brahman are eternally distinct. The jiva (individual soul) is always dependent on and subordinate to Brahman.

From Chandogya Upanishad (8.3.4): "This Atman of mine within the heart is smaller than a rice grain, smaller than a barley corn, smaller than a mustard seed, smaller than a grain of millet, smaller even than the kernel of a grain of millet. This Atman of mine within the heart is greater than the earth, greater than the atmosphere, greater than the sky, greater than all these worlds."

MAYA — The Creative Power of Illusion

Sanskrit: माया

Definition: Maya is the cosmic principle by which the One (Brahman) appears as the many (the phenomenal world). It is the power of manifestation, and simultaneously the source of ignorance (avidya) that makes us mistake the apparent for the real.

Maya is described as:
- Anirvachaniya — indescribable (neither real nor unreal)
- Mithya — not absolute reality, but not mere nothingness either (like a dream that feels real)
- The projective power that creates the appearance of diversity

Maya operates through three Gunas:
1. Tamas — inertia, darkness, dullness (creates veiling/avarana)
2. Rajas — activity, passion, desire (creates projection/viksepa)
3. Sattva — clarity, purity, harmony (nearest to pure consciousness)

Shankaracharya: "Brahma satyam jagan mithya, jivo brahmaiva naparah" — Brahman alone is real, the world is an appearance, the individual soul is none other than Brahman.

SAMSARA — The Cycle of Existence

Sanskrit: संसार

Definition: Samsara (literally "flowing together" or "wandering") is the cycle of death and rebirth that the individual soul undergoes due to karma and avidya (ignorance). It is the state of bondage that moksha releases one from.

The Vedic view of samsara:
- The soul (jiva) takes birth repeatedly based on accumulated karma
- Each birth provides opportunities for spiritual growth
- Desire (kama) and aversion (dvesha) drive the cycle
- The ultimate purpose of samsara is the soul's evolution toward liberation

Bhagavad Gita (15.7): "The living entities in this conditioned world are My eternal fragmental parts. Due to conditioned life, they are struggling very hard with the six senses, which include the mind."

THE FOUR VEDAS — Shruti (Revealed Knowledge)

The Vedas are the oldest and most authoritative scriptures of Vedic tradition, considered Apaurusheya (not of human authorship) — they were "heard" (shruti) by ancient seers (rishis) in deep states of meditation.

1. RIGVEDA — The Veda of Praise
   - Oldest Veda, composed approximately 1500-1200 BCE
   - 1,028 hymns (suktas) organized in 10 mandalas (books)
   - Primarily hymns to Vedic deities: Indra, Agni, Varuna, Soma, Ushas, etc.
   - Contains the Purusha Sukta (hymn of the Cosmic Person) and Nasadiya Sukta (creation hymn)

2. SAMAVEDA — The Veda of Melodies
   - Set most of its verses to music; the source of classical Indian music
   - Primarily a liturgical text for the Udgatar (chanting priest) at Soma sacrifices
   - Nearly all its verses are derived from the Rigveda but set to specific melodies

3. YAJURVEDA — The Veda of Ritual Formulas
   - Contains prose and verse formulas (yajus) used by the Adhvaryu priest
   - Two major recensions: Krishna (Black) Yajurveda and Shukla (White) Yajurveda
   - Includes Taittiriya Samhita, Brihadaranyaka Upanishad, and Isha Upanishad

4. ATHARVAVEDA — The Veda of Magical Formulas
   - Contains hymns for healing, protection, prosperity, and philosophy
   - More folk-oriented; preserves ancient traditions of Ayurveda and Jyotisha
   - The Mundaka, Mandukya, and Prashna Upanishads belong to this Veda

Each Veda has four sections:
- Samhitas: The core hymns and mantras
- Brahmanas: Prose texts explaining ritual procedures
- Aranyakas: Forest treatises on meditation and symbolism
- Upanishads: Philosophical and spiritual teachings (Vedanta)

THE SIX ORTHODOX SCHOOLS (Shad Darshana)

The six classical systems of Vedic philosophy (all accept the authority of the Vedas):

1. NYAYA (Gautama): Logic and epistemology. Defines four valid means of knowledge: pratyaksha (perception), anumana (inference), upamana (comparison), and shabda (testimony). Theistic system arguing for God's existence through logic.

2. VAISHESHIKA (Kanada): Atomistic philosophy. The universe consists of atomic substances (padartha). Developed sophisticated ontological categories.

3. SAMKHYA (Kapila): Dualistic system of Purusha (consciousness) and Prakriti (matter/nature). The 25 principles (tattvas) of manifestation. Non-theistic.

4. YOGA (Patanjali): The practical complement to Samkhya. Accepts Samkhya metaphysics but adds Ishvara (God) as a special Purusha and provides the eightfold path.

5. MIMAMSA (Jaimini): Philosophy of ritual action. The Vedas are eternal and self-validating; dharma is known through Vedic injunctions. Foundation for understanding karma-kanda.

6. VEDANTA (Badarayana/Vyasa): Philosophy of the Upanishads. Centered on Brahman as ultimate reality. Has three main sub-schools: Advaita (Shankara), Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja), and Dvaita (Madhva).
