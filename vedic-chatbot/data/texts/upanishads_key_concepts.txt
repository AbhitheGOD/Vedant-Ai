[SOURCE: Brihadaranyaka Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 1] [VERSE: 3.28]
Asato ma sadgamaya | Tamaso ma jyotirgamaya | Mrityor ma amritam gamaya ||

Translation: Lead me from the unreal to the Real. Lead me from darkness to Light. Lead me from death to Immortality.

Commentary: This ancient prayer from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (1.3.28) is one of the most beloved mantras in Vedic tradition. It expresses the fundamental aspiration of every spiritual seeker — to move from ignorance (asat, tamas) toward true knowledge, light, and liberation from the cycle of birth and death.

[SOURCE: Chandogya Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 6] [VERSE: 8.7]
Tat tvam asi — That Thou Art.

Sanskrit: तत् त्वम् असि

Commentary: This is one of the four Mahavakyas (great sayings) of the Upanishads, found in the Chandogya Upanishad. The teacher Uddalaka Aruni tells his son Shvetaketu: Brahman (the ultimate reality, "Tat") is identical with the individual self (Atman, "tvam"). The recognition of this identity is the very heart of Advaita Vedanta (non-dualistic philosophy).

[SOURCE: Mandukya Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 1] [VERSE: 2]
Ayam atma brahma — This Self is Brahman.

Sanskrit: अयम् आत्मा ब्रह्म

Commentary: The second Mahavakya from the Mandukya Upanishad. The entire Mandukya Upanishad consists of just 12 verses analyzing consciousness through four states — waking (jagrat), dreaming (svapna), deep sleep (sushupti), and turiya (the fourth, pure witnessing consciousness beyond the three states). The Self (Atman) in all its states is identical with the Absolute (Brahman).

[SOURCE: Aitareya Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 3] [VERSE: 3]
Prajnanam Brahma — Consciousness is Brahman.

Sanskrit: प्रज्ञानं ब्रह्म

Commentary: Third Mahavakya from the Aitareya Upanishad (Rigveda tradition). Pure consciousness (prajna) is the fundamental nature of reality. This teaching dissolves the distinction between knower, knowledge, and known — all are expressions of one undivided consciousness.

[SOURCE: Taittiriya Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 2] [VERSE: 1]
Aham Brahmasmi — I am Brahman.

Sanskrit: अहम् ब्रह्मास्मि

Commentary: Fourth Mahavakya from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (though associated with the Taittiriya tradition). It is the first-person realization of the identity of Atman and Brahman. This is not ego-assertion but the dissolution of the individual ego into infinite consciousness.

[SOURCE: Isha Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 1] [VERSE: 1]
Isavasyam idam sarvam yat kinca jagatyam jagat | Tena tyaktena bhunjitha ma gridhah kasya sviddhanam ||

Translation: All this — whatever exists in this changing universe — should be covered by the Lord. Enjoy what He gives you with detachment; do not covet anyone's wealth.

Sanskrit: ईशावास्यमिदं सर्वं यत्किञ्च जगत्यां जगत् | तेन त्यक्तेन भुञ्जीथा मा गृधः कस्यस्विद्धनम् ||

Commentary: The opening verse of the Isha Upanishad (also called Ishavasya Upanishad) sets the tone for Vedantic understanding. The entire cosmos is pervaded by the Divine. True enjoyment comes through renunciation and non-covetousness — not through possessiveness but through offering everything back to its source.

[SOURCE: Kena Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 1] [VERSE: 1-2]
Willed by whom does the directed mind go towards its object? Being directed by whom does the vital breath, the first cause, proceed? By whom is this speech willed that people utter? Who is the god that directs the eye and the ear?

Commentary: The Kena Upanishad opens with a series of questions pointing to the ultimate knower behind all knowing — the Brahman that cannot be known as an object because It is the very subject of all knowledge. "That which is not thought by the mind, but by which the mind thinks — know That alone as Brahman."

[SOURCE: Mundaka Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 2] [VERSE: 2.5]
Two birds, inseparable companions, dwell in the same tree. One of them eats the fruits of the tree; the other, though it does not eat, observes.

Sanskrit: द्वा सुपर्णा सयुजा सखाया समानं वृक्षं परिषस्वजाते | तयोरन्यः पिप्पलं स्वाद्वत्त्यनश्नन्नन्यो अभिचाकशीति ||

Commentary: This profound metaphor from the Mundaka Upanishad (also found in Rigveda 1.164.20) describes the individual soul (jiva) and the Supreme Soul (Paramatman) dwelling together in the body (tree). The jiva eats the fruits (experiences pleasures and pains), while the Paramatman merely witnesses without attachment.

[SOURCE: Taittiriya Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 2] [VERSE: 1-5]
The Pancha Kosha (Five Sheaths) doctrine:
1. Annamaya Kosha — the physical body made of food
2. Pranamaya Kosha — the vital/breath body
3. Manomaya Kosha — the mental/emotional body
4. Vijnanamaya Kosha — the intellectual/discriminative body
5. Anandamaya Kosha — the bliss body

Commentary: The Taittiriya Upanishad presents the teaching of five sheaths (koshas) that cover the true Self. Spiritual practice involves progressively transcending each sheath to realize the pure Atman that lies beyond all of them. This framework is foundational to yogic anatomy and Vedantic self-inquiry.

[SOURCE: Katha Upanishad] [CHAPTER: 1] [VERSE: 3.3-4]
Know the Atman as the Lord of the chariot, the body as the chariot, the intellect as the charioteer, and the mind as the reins. The senses, say the wise, are the horses; the objects of the senses are the paths. He who has understanding, whose mind is always held firm — his senses are under control, like the good horses of a charioteer.

Sanskrit: आत्मानं रथिनं विद्धि शरीरं रथमेव तु | बुद्धिं तु सारथिं विद्धि मनः प्रग्रहमेव च || इन्द्रियाणि हयानाहुर्विषयांस्तेषु गोचरान् | आत्मेन्द्रियमनोयुक्तं भोक्तेत्याहुर्मनीषिणः ||

Commentary: The chariot metaphor from Katha Upanishad (later elaborated in Plato and used in the Mahabharata). The Self is the passenger, body is the chariot, intellect the charioteer, mind the reins, and senses the horses. Without a trained intellect (viveka), the horses (senses) will run wild. This is the foundation of Yoga psychology.
