[SOURCE: Yoga Sutras of Patanjali] [CHAPTER: 1] [VERSE: 1-2]
Atha yoganushasanam | Yogas chitta vritti nirodhah ||

Translation: Now begins the teaching of Yoga. Yoga is the cessation of the fluctuations (vrittis) of the mind (chitta).

Sanskrit: अथ योगानुशासनम् | योगश्चित्तवृत्तिनिरोधः ||

Commentary: The second sutra is arguably the most important definition in all of yoga philosophy. Patanjali defines yoga not as postures or breathing exercises, but as the complete stilling of the mental modifications. When the mind's constant flux ceases, the seer (drashta) rests in their own true nature.

[SOURCE: Yoga Sutras of Patanjali] [CHAPTER: 1] [VERSE: 3-4]
Tada drashtuh svarupe avasthanam | Vritti sarupyam itaratra ||

Translation: Then the Seer abides in his own nature. At other times, the Seer is identified with the modifications of the mind.

Commentary: When yoga (mental stillness) is achieved, the pure consciousness (purusha) recognizes itself as the Seer, distinct from the seen. In ordinary consciousness, we mistakenly identify with our thoughts, emotions, and sensations — this is called avidya (ignorance), the root of all suffering.

[SOURCE: Yoga Sutras of Patanjali] [CHAPTER: 1] [VERSE: 12-14]
Abhyasa vairagyabhyam tannirodhah | Tatra sthitau yatno abhyasah | Sa tu dirgha kala nairantarya satkara asevito dridha bhumih ||

Translation: That (cessation) comes through practice (abhyasa) and detachment (vairagya). Practice is the effort to be there (in stillness). This practice becomes firmly grounded when cultivated with dedication for a long time, without interruption, and with an attitude of devotion.

Commentary: The two wings of yoga — abhyasa (consistent practice) and vairagya (detachment from the fruits of practice and from worldly attractions). Neither extreme asceticism nor worldly indulgence, but balanced, dedicated practice over a long period.

[SOURCE: Yoga Sutras of Patanjali] [CHAPTER: 2] [VERSE: 1-2]
Tapah svadhyaya ishvarapranidhana kriya yogah | Samadhib havana arthah klesha tanu karanarthash cha ||

Translation: Tapas (austerity/purification), Svadhyaya (self-study/scriptural study), and Ishvara Pranidhana (surrender to God) constitute Kriya Yoga. (Kriya Yoga is for) cultivating samadhi and attenuating the kleshas.

Commentary: Kriya Yoga — the yoga of action — is Patanjali's practical framework. Tapas purifies the body and senses; Svadhyaya develops self-knowledge through scripture and introspection; Ishvara Pranidhana dissolves the ego through devotion.

[SOURCE: Yoga Sutras of Patanjali] [CHAPTER: 2] [VERSE: 3]
Avidya asmita raga dvesha abhinivesha kleshas ||

Translation: The five kleshas (afflictions/obstacles) are: avidya (ignorance), asmita (ego/I-sense), raga (attachment), dvesha (aversion), and abhinivesha (fear of death/clinging to life).

Commentary: Patanjali identifies five root causes of human suffering. Avidya (ignorance of our true nature) is the root klesha from which all others spring. The entire yogic path is aimed at dissolving these five afflictions through practice and discrimination (viveka).

[SOURCE: Yoga Sutras of Patanjali] [CHAPTER: 2] [VERSE: 29]
Yama niyama asana pranayama pratyahara dharana dhyana samadhibhyo ashtau angani ||

Translation: The eight limbs (ashtanga) are: Yama (ethical restraints), Niyama (observances), Asana (posture), Pranayama (breath regulation), Pratyahara (sense withdrawal), Dharana (concentration), Dhyana (meditation), and Samadhi (absorption).

Commentary: Patanjali's Ashtanga Yoga — the eight-limbed path. The first two limbs (Yama and Niyama) are ethical foundations. Asana and Pranayama prepare the body-mind. Pratyahara withdraws awareness from external stimuli. The last three — Dharana, Dhyana, Samadhi — together constitute Samyama, the inner path to direct knowledge.

[SOURCE: Yoga Sutras of Patanjali] [CHAPTER: 2] [VERSE: 30-31]
Ahimsa satya asteya brahmacharya aparigrahah yamah | Jati desha kala samaya anavacchinnah sarva bhaumah mahavratam ||

Translation: The Yamas are: Ahimsa (non-violence), Satya (truthfulness), Asteya (non-stealing), Brahmacharya (continence/right use of energy), and Aparigraha (non-covetousness). These, not limited by class, place, time, or circumstance, constitute the Great Vow.

Commentary: The five Yamas are called the "Mahavrata" — the Great Vow — because they are universal moral principles, not relative codes of conduct. They apply to everyone, in every situation. Ahimsa (non-violence) is primary among them.

[SOURCE: Yoga Sutras of Patanjali] [CHAPTER: 3] [VERSE: 1-3]
Desha bandhas chittasya dharana | Tatra pratyaya ekatanata dhyanam | Tad eva artha matra nirbhasam svarupa shunyam iva samadhi ||

Translation: Concentration (Dharana) is binding the mind to one place. Meditation (Dhyana) is the continuous flow of awareness toward that object. When only the object shines forth, as if devoid of one's own form, that is Samadhi.

Commentary: The three inner limbs of yoga — dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), and samadhi (absorption) — form a continuum. In dharana, attention returns to the object repeatedly; in dhyana, it flows continuously; in samadhi, the meditator and the meditated merge, and individuality temporarily dissolves.

[SOURCE: Yoga Sutras of Patanjali] [CHAPTER: 4] [VERSE: 34]
Purusha artha shunyanam gunanam pratiprasavah kaivalyam svarupa pratishtha va chiti shaktir iti ||

Translation: Kaivalya (liberation) is the return of the Gunas into the unmanifest state, there being no further purpose for the Purusha. Or, it is the establishment of the power of pure consciousness in its own nature.

Commentary: The final sutra describes Kaivalya — the ultimate goal of Yoga. The three Gunas (Sattva, Rajas, Tamas) that constitute Prakriti (nature/matter) dissolve when they have served their purpose of experience and liberation. Pure Consciousness (Purusha) stands alone, shining in its own light — free, unbound, perfect.
